CLAIMSDOCK
A production AI copilot for medical-claims adjudication, built and shipped solo — Claude and Kimi K2.6 running the same system side by side, so I could measure the tradeoff instead of assuming it.

Role: Product direction, spec authorship, system architecture, research, testing, deployment.
Stack: Next.js, TypeScript, Anthropic's Claude, Moonshot's Kimi K2.6, self-hosted embeddings, deployed on Vercel.


THE SHORT VERSION

ClaimsDock reads incoming medical claims, gathers evidence, rates its own confidence, and recommends an action — approve, deny, escalate, or hold for missing information. Every recommendation cites a real source document. Nothing becomes a real decision without a human clicking to make it one. I built the whole system — the evaluation pipeline, the retrieval layer, an agentic router with a grounded assistant panel, and a human-approval gate with its own AI-checked guardrails — then tested two different frontier models against each other on the same task before deciding which one to trust in production.

A few things worth knowing before the rest of this:

- I ran Claude and Kimi head-to-head on the same claims and designed the metric that actually mattered — not raw accuracy, but how often a wrong answer would slip through undetected. Full results and the reasoning behind the metric are below, with a data visualization covering the whole comparison.
- I designed the interface myself, from a rough early sketch through five explored directions down to three genuinely distinct visual styles, each in light and dark, checked to WCAG AAA contrast computationally rather than by eye.
- I grounded a specific design decision — how Anchor's confidence tiers are worded — in peer-reviewed research on when people actually trust algorithmic advice, not intuition alone.
- I found and fixed three real bugs the day this went to production, none of which had ever shown up in months of local development. That gap is exactly why the deployment gets its own section below.
- I refined one AI guardrail across eleven documented rounds of live failure before realizing the actual fix was structural, not another prompt tweak — then built a permanent test suite from that lesson.

The rest of this case study walks through how each piece works, the decisions behind them, and what I'd tell you if you asked what a real, deployed, multi-provider AI system actually takes to build. A condensed, phase-by-phase build log covers the full process in more detail than fits here; I'll point to it where it's worth a deeper look.


THE PROBLEM

Most insurance claims are simple. A small number are genuinely ambiguous, missing information, or fraudulent, and those are the ones that eat an adjuster's day — not because the work is hard, but because sorting the merely-flagged from the actually-wrong takes real judgment, every time, at volume. I wanted to build something that handles the sorting honestly: surface what's genuinely uncertain, ground every claim in real evidence and real policy language, and leave the decision itself with a person who can be held accountable for it. Not an autopilot. A system that does the reading so a human can do the deciding faster, and with better information than they'd have otherwise.

I chose medical claims specifically because it's a domain with real regulatory stakes (ERISA governs how a denial has to be justified), real fraud patterns worth naming correctly, and enough structural complexity — deductibles, network status, benefit caps, linked claims — to make "just ask the model" an obviously wrong approach on its own. The same architecture would re-skin for other high-stakes review work; I picked this domain because it's unforgiving of shortcuts.


SYSTEMS THINKING: FOUR PIECES, ONE SET OF RULES

I designed ClaimsDock as four systems that share a small number of hard rules rather than one AI system with a few features bolted on:

- An Evaluation Pipeline that reads every incoming claim automatically and produces evidence, a confidence tier, and a recommendation — no human involved yet.
- A Router and an embedded assistant, Anchor, that an adjuster can ask a question of at any time, in plain language, grounded in the same evidence and the same policy documents.
- A Human Gate that sits between any AI output and any real action, backed by its own guardrails.
- A retrieval layer, shared by both the Pipeline and Anchor, that grounds everything either of them says in three real reference documents instead of the model's own memory.

The rule that ties all four together, and the one I held to the most strictly through the whole build: calculations stay calculations. Severity, SLA math, coverage math, and the category-to-recommended-action mapping are all deterministic code, not a model's judgment call. I made this decision in the very first planning pass, before any pipeline code existed, specifically because the alternative — asking a model to compute a dollar amount or a deadline — trades a testable function for a probabilistic one with no upside. A model earns its keep by reading messy evidence and judging genuinely ambiguous things. It has no business re-deriving arithmetic a spreadsheet could do correctly every time.


THE EVALUATION PIPELINE: TWO CALLS, NOT ONE

Every claim goes through exactly two model calls, and I designed it that way on purpose. The first call reads the claim — plus its linked claim, if it's part of a professional/facility pair, plus the billing provider's recent volume history if a spike pattern is relevant — and produces raw evidence and a proposed category: clean, ambiguous, missing data, complex coverage math, or fraudulent. It does not rate its own confidence, and it does not recommend an action.

The second call is shown only that evidence and category — never the first call's own reasoning, never the original claim again — and independently judges a confidence tier before drafting the recommendation. I split these into two isolated calls specifically to avoid a model grading its own work in the same breath it did the work. A single call that finds a pattern and immediately rates how sure it is of that pattern is checking its own homework. Two calls, with the second one blind to the first one's reasoning, is a real independent judgment instead.

That confidence tier matters more than it might look. Anchor's four tiers — High Confidence, Confident, Suspected, Uncertain — describe how directly the evidence supports a conclusion, not a bare probability score. I made this distinction deliberately after reading research on how people actually respond to disclosed algorithm accuracy (more on that below): a number invites false precision, while a tier tied to the strength of the actual evidence is something a human can reason about the same way they'd reason about a colleague's confidence.

The claim set grew substantially over the course of the build — from 20 authored claims to 132, covering clean, ambiguous, missing-data, complex-math, and fraud scenarios in a distribution I negotiated deliberately rather than left arbitrary. At that scale, a single Pipeline call per batch stopped being viable outright — a real, uncached run against the full set timed out past both the model's own practical limits and this project's own request ceiling. I chunked the claim set into batches sized dynamically off the total claim count, with a continuous-cursor distribution algorithm that keeps every batch's category mix — and therefore its token cost — roughly even. Getting that distribution right took three real, live-tested attempts, not one: a naive contiguous slice clustered similar claims together and made per-batch variance worse; a binary clean/non-clean split fixed one call's variance while making the other one worse; the actual fix, verified with a unit test before spending another live API call on it, concatenates every category's claims in file order and round-robins a single continuous cursor across the whole set, never resetting between categories. At 132 claims, that's six batches of 22, and every batch gets its own retry if a single one fails transiently — a real gap I found and fixed after watching one flaky network timeout on one batch discard five other batches' already-succeeded, already-paid-for results.

The Pipeline runs on Kimi K2.6 in production. I made that call after real, repeated head-to-head testing against Claude, not by default — the full reasoning and the numbers are in the Claude vs. Kimi section below.


GROUNDING: RETRIEVAL, CHUNKING, AND THE HONEST LIMITS OF EMBEDDING SEARCH

Every recommendation ClaimsDock makes traces back to one of three reference documents I wrote myself: a Coverage and Adjudication Policy (deductible logic, network rates, prior-authorization rules, benefit caps — entirely synthetic but structurally realistic), a Fraud-Indicator Reference (real, standard industry terminology for billing fraud, sourced from OIG enforcement history, DOJ False Claims Act settlements, and CMS's own coding-integrity program), and a Regulatory Deadline Reference covering ERISA's actual tiered decision deadlines. Anchor and the Pipeline both cite these by name; nothing gets invented on the fly.

I built the retrieval layer on local, self-hosted embeddings — Hugging Face's own transformers.js port (the `@huggingface/transformers` package, running the `Xenova/all-MiniLM-L6-v2` model) — rather than a hosted embedding API, specifically to avoid a second external API dependency for something this corpus is small enough not to need one for. Each of the three documents gets chunked on its own natural section headings, embedded once, and held in a plain in-memory array. No hosted vector database — at a few dozen chunks total, comparing a query's embedding against every chunk directly is instant, and a hosted database would be solving a scale problem this project doesn't have.

Retrieval quality turned out to be its own real, ongoing tradeoff, not a solved problem I could set and forget. A live smoke test against genuine questions — not synthetic fixtures — found that the exact chunk containing ERISA's own tiered-deadline table ranked fifth of six results for the one question it was built to answer. The chunk was built almost entirely from raw markdown table syntax, which a sentence-embedding model, trained overwhelmingly on prose, embeds poorly. Flattening the table into plain-language sentences barely moved the ranking, which told me the real problem was structural: mean-pooled embeddings dilute a long chunk's vector toward its average content, letting a short, keyword-dense chunk systematically out-rank a longer one that actually contains the right answer — a genuine, well-documented limitation of embedding search on its own, not a bug specific to this corpus. I fixed it with hybrid scoring, blending embedding similarity (70%) with a lightweight keyword-overlap signal (30%), which recovered the ERISA chunk to second place. I left the ranking imperfect past that point on purpose — Anchor and the Pipeline both retrieve several top-ranked chunks for real synthesis, not just the single top result, so a small ranking margin doesn't produce a wrong answer even when the ranking itself isn't perfect. Tuning retrieval further would have chased diminishing returns against a problem the architecture already tolerates.


THE ROUTER AND ANCHOR: AN AGENTIC LOOP, NOT A CHATBOT WRAPPER

Anchor is the part of ClaimsDock an adjuster actually talks to — a plain-language question box that can look up a claim, re-analyze it, draft a recommendation, or answer a general policy question, entirely independent of whatever the automatic Pipeline already decided. Underneath it is a real agentic tool-use loop: a Router classifies each question into one of four tools and extracts the right parameters, executes the tool, and — critically — can chain a second tool call if the first one's result implies a follow-up is needed, inside a bounded round budget rather than a fixed one-shot call.

I designed against three distinct, separately testable failure modes, because they're genuinely different problems with different fixes: the Router choosing the wrong tool entirely; choosing the right tool but extracting the wrong parameters from it (the right kind of analysis, pointed at the wrong claim); and, specific to general policy questions, misjudging whether a question is about one specific claim or nothing in particular. Tool-choice accuracy and parameter-extraction accuracy are not the same failure mode and don't get fixed the same way — a wrong tool choice is a routing-prompt problem, while a right tool with wrong parameters is usually a context problem, like the Router defaulting to whichever claim happened to be on screen instead of a claim named explicitly in the question. I tested both separately with an adversarial question set built specifically to probe each one, not just the happy path, and found and fixed a real bug on the first live run: a question that genuinely needed two tools in sequence (claim facts, then a policy fact) had its second tool call silently dropped, because the original round-trip only handled a single dispatch round. I fixed that as a bounded loop rather than assuming every real question resolves in exactly one call — the kind of assumption that looks reasonable until a user asks a genuinely compound question.

Anchor runs on either Claude or Kimi, switchable from a settings toggle, independent of whichever provider the automatic Pipeline uses to evaluate claims — a decision I made deliberately, since Anchor's own answer quality turned out to be worth testing on its own terms rather than assuming it would just track the Pipeline's provider choice. And Anchor's memory survives that switch: ask a follow-up question after flipping from Kimi to Claude mid-conversation, and Anchor still knows what "that claim" refers to, because the one turn of prior-question memory it carries is stored independent of which model answers it. That's a small detail, but it's the kind of detail that either works invisibly or breaks a user's trust the first time it doesn't — I made sure it was the former.


CLAUDE VS. KIMI: A REAL EXPERIMENT, NOT A FEATURE CHECKLIST

I didn't pick a model provider by reputation. I built a real comparison harness and ran both providers against the identical, fixed claim set across repeated matched batches, then designed the metric I actually cared about before trusting either provider's raw accuracy number.

Raw accuracy treats every wrong category as equally bad, and it isn't equally bad. A model that gets a claim's category wrong but stays cautious about it still reaches a human for review — the deterministic layer routes anything below a confident tier to a person regardless of category. The dangerous failure is different: a wrong "clean" call paired with a confident tier, which auto-resolves with nobody ever reviewing it. I called this a silent miss and tracked it specifically, since it's the one failure mode this system's own safety architecture can't catch after the fact. It mattered more to the actual routing decision than the accuracy number did.

Confirmed across two independent matched-prompt batches each: Claude averaged 92% category accuracy with a 5% silent-miss rate, but completed a full batch cleanly only about four times out of five. Kimi averaged 82% accuracy with a 13% silent-miss rate, but finished cleanly on every single run tested. That's a real, opposite tradeoff, not a win for either model outright — Claude is more accurate and safer when it finishes; Kimi finishes more reliably. I made the call to route the live Pipeline to Kimi in production, in writing, as a cost-driven decision appropriate to a public prototype with no real patients or dollars on the line — and documented explicitly that an actual production system, with real stakes attached to an 8-point accuracy gap, would be permitted to make a different call than I did here. I built a standalone data visualization covering the full comparison — the accuracy arc across every prompt iteration I tested, the reliability gap, and the silent-miss breakdown side by side for both providers.

One thing I noticed hands-on, past the numbers: Kimi's answers inside Anchor were, if anything, more thorough than Claude's for the same questions, and it used real generated tables and bulleted structure to lay out a grid of information far more often than Claude did — Claude consistently fell back to prose with the occasional bullet or numbered list for the same kind of request. That's not a metric I designed a test around, just something worth knowing if you're evaluating either model for a task where structured output actually helps a reader.


THE HUMAN GATE: WHERE AI OUTPUT MEETS A REAL DECISION

Nothing in ClaimsDock becomes a real action — an approval, a denial, an escalation — without a human clicking to make it happen, and I built two guardrails specifically for the moment that click happens. The first is deterministic and costs nothing: it compares whatever action an adjuster chooses against the Pipeline's own recommendation and surfaces a soft confirmation, citing the evidence already on file, if the two disagree. It never blocks anyone from proceeding with their own choice — a system that could override a human's own decision would contradict the entire premise the platform rests on.

The second guardrail is where most of the real engineering difficulty in this whole build lived. Denying a claim requires four structured justification fields — the specific reason, the plan or policy provision it violates, any internal standard applied, and what could reverse the decision — and a model-backed check grades that justification's coherence against the claim's own evidence before it's accepted, drafting a better version when it falls short. I refined this single guardrail across eleven documented rounds of real, live failure: a wrong document cited, a fabricated policy provision, a genuine gap in my own reference corpus that no amount of prompt tuning could paper over, a retrieval-recall problem where the same correct citation would rank differently depending on how clinically-worded a claim's evidence happened to read, and — twice — two of my own guardrail rules directly contradicting each other on different claims. The actual fix, once I stepped back far enough to see it, wasn't another prompt patch. One of the four fields was never a genuine judgment call in the first place: a denial for fraud only ever follows one specific confidence path, so the correct policy citation for that field is always the same fixed text. I removed it from judgment entirely rather than keep asking a model to independently rediscover an answer that never varied. I built a permanent regression suite directly from that saga's own lesson — every one of those eleven rounds was checked only against the single claim that had just failed, with nothing verifying a fix didn't silently break a claim that had already passed, and the suite caught exactly that kind of regression on its very first run.

Every status change, override, escalation, and denial writes to an audit log with who, when, and why — a flat, queryable array specifically so a real question like "show me every claim approved in March" is answerable directly, not reconstructed after the fact. A claim's current status is always just its latest log entry, so there's no separate status value that could quietly drift out of sync with the record of how it got there. The condensed build log covers the full eleven-round guardrail account in more depth than belongs here, including the two rounds where a live domain-logic disagreement — whether disproving a fraud theory counts as a legitimate reversal path or an invalid contradiction — genuinely needed to be reasoned through rather than assumed either way.


THE INTERFACE: SIX REAL DESIGN COMBINATIONS, ONE TOKEN SYSTEM

I designed ClaimsDock's interface myself, starting from a plain layout I sketched early on — a masthead, a claims table, and a side panel of mini-cards for the assistant — long before any of the backend architecture above existed as running code. That rough shape became the seed for five real style directions, explored side by side rather than picked from a single default. I narrowed those five to three, and I was deliberate about what narrowing meant here: not picking a winner and discarding the rest, but keeping the three that each carried a genuinely different point of view on the same underlying data, not just a different palette on the same layout.

Ledger reads dense and tabular, built for a high-volume queue where information density is the point. Clinical is serif-forward and precision-first, closer to how an actual clinical record reads. Field is warmer and more approachable, built for a less adversarial read of the same claims. Each of the three ships in both light and dark — six real combinations total — and every one of them is driven by a single token architecture rather than six separately hand-maintained copies. A style-and-brightness pair is one entry in a shared color and type system, so adding a seventh combination later means adding one token block, not rewriting six sets of component styles by hand.

I held the whole system to WCAG AAA contrast, the strictest tier, at 7:1, and I checked it computationally rather than eyeballing it — which caught three real failures I wouldn't have caught by looking: accent text against its own ink color dropping as low as 4.77:1 in Clinical light and 6.98:1 in Ledger dark. I fixed both at the primitive-token level rather than patching the individual components that happened to expose them, so the fix propagated to every place that token gets used at once, not just the two spots where I'd actually noticed a problem.

Wiring the real design into working React surfaced genuine engineering bugs, not just visual polish work. A shared fade-in and fade-out animation was starting its exit sequence from inside a React effect, which only runs after the browser has already committed a frame — meaning a modal could sit fully removed from the page for one real paint before the effect ever got a chance to fade it back in, and claims with more content on them made that gap far more likely to actually be seen. I traced it to React's own commit timing and fixed it by deriving the transition synchronously during render instead of reacting to it after the fact. Separately, a status-and-severity legend popover was getting clipped at the bottom of a short table — not a popover bug at all, but a table container that had set horizontal scrolling without setting vertical scrolling explicitly, which CSS quietly resolves to the same clipping behavior by default. I fixed it by rendering the popover through a portal instead of fighting the table's own overflow rules.

None of this was a single generation I approved and moved on from. The interface went through real, hands-on iteration over a genuinely large amount of work — the kind that doesn't show up as a line in a spec document, but is exactly the layer an adjuster actually spends their whole day looking at.


RESEARCH: WHY DENISE'S SKEPTICISM ISN'T A CHARACTER QUIRK

I built ClaimsDock's interface around a persona, Denise Marsh — a senior claims adjuster with nine years in the field, the kind of adjuster who gets routed the genuinely disputed cases rather than routine ones. Her caution toward automated recommendations isn't invented color. It's grounded in real, peer-reviewed research, and that research directly shaped decisions I made in the product, not just the persona.

A key tension I designed against is well-documented: people trust algorithms readily on analytical tasks, but that trust drops sharply the moment they see the algorithm make a mistake — more sharply than the same mistake made by a human — and they lean back on their own judgment afterward. Researchers call this algorithm aversion, and it's been studied enough that its boundaries are known too: letting people edit an algorithm's output, rather than just accept or reject it, measurably softens the effect. That finding is a direct line to the Human Gate's "Approve with edit" path, which exists specifically so an adjuster never has to reject a correct finding outright just because one number in it is wrong.

One finding changed how I thought about Anchor's confidence tiers specifically: reliance tracks an algorithm's stated accuracy right up until it's actually wrong more often than it said it would be — at which point trust doesn't just decline, it collapses. That's a much narrower target than "show a confidence score." It means Anchor's tiers have to be genuinely accurate, not just present. An honest "Very confident" that turns out wrong, or a "Suspected" that turns out right more often than not, is worse for trust than showing no tier at all — which is exactly why I built the tiers around how directly the evidence actually supports a conclusion, not a number invented to look precise.

The same research shaped the Human Gate's design in a second way: people aren't blanket-trusting or blanket-rejecting an algorithm's suggestions. They lean on the correct ones more than the incorrect ones, meaning they're actively curating a recommendation, not just accepting it. Anchor's citation-plus-disclaimer pattern is my own version of the well-timed nudge that same research points toward — not proof it works, just a design decision built on real evidence about how people actually calibrate trust, rather than a guess.

The most uncomfortable finding, and the one I take most seriously, challenges an assumption I might otherwise have made by default: that putting a human in the approval path guarantees real oversight. The "Zombies in the Loop?" research tests that assumption directly and finds it doesn't reliably hold — people accepted AI-generated advice even when told the AI's training data was unknown or presumably biased, and even when explicitly warned to be skeptical of it. A human checkpoint can become a rubber stamp if the human isn't actually made to engage with the decision. That finding is the entire reason denying a claim in ClaimsDock isn't a single click — it requires the four-field justification described above, checked for real coherence against the claim's own evidence, catching cases where a stated reason cited the wrong fraud category even though the underlying denial itself was correct. The goal was never just a human in the loop. It's a human who has to actually do something in that loop.

Sourcing note: the algorithm-aversion and trust-calibration findings above come from peer-reviewed academic research (Behaviour and Information Technology, 2025, and an academic preprint on human-in-the-loop overtrust); an industry source covering a 2025 claims-management conference contributed real-world context — workforce turnover pressure in claims organizations specifically — weighted below the academic material and never treated as equivalent to it.


PRODUCTION: WHAT ONLY A REAL DEPLOYMENT ACTUALLY TESTS

I built and tested every piece of ClaimsDock locally before deploying it, and the deployment itself still surfaced three real bugs, in order, none of which had ever shown up in months of local development. That gap is the whole reason a real deployment is worth doing rather than skipping — a genuinely different runtime environment (stateless functions, a read-only filesystem, real cold starts) tests things local development structurally can't.

A typo'd environment variable name surfaced first, as a credential error from the wrong SDK entirely — Kimi's client silently falls back to checking for an OpenAI key when its own resolves empty, which took a real log dive to trace back to a one-character typo. Second, and harder to find: the local embedding library's native binary wasn't reaching the deployed serverless function at all, since Next.js's automatic file-tracing doesn't reliably catch a shared library that's loaded dynamically at runtime instead of imported statically — my first fix wasn't sufficient on its own, confirmed live, and I had to add an explicit include for the exact routes that needed it before it actually worked. Third, once retrieval got past that: the same library's default cache directory lives inside a folder that's read-only on Vercel at runtime, redirected to the one writable path a serverless function actually has. None of these are exotic bugs. They're exactly the kind of environment-specific gap that never shows up until you actually ship, which is the whole argument for shipping early and for real rather than treating "it works on my machine" as the finish line.

The Pipeline's own results are cached per calendar week and shared across every visitor, since the output depends only on claim data, not on who's asking — meaning only the first visitor after a weekly cache rollover pays for a live run, and I closed even that gap with a scheduled job that pre-warms the cache before real traffic arrives.


WHAT THIS DEMONSTRATES

ClaimsDock is a real, deployed system, not a demo that only works in a controlled walkthrough. It reflects the way I actually approach a build: decide the architecture on paper before writing code, treat a model as the tool for genuine judgment calls and deterministic code as the tool for everything else, test a real tradeoff instead of assuming an answer, and verify a fix against the environment it actually has to survive in — including, more than once in this build, catching my own assumption before it became a shipped mistake.
