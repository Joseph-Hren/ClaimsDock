# Fraud-Indicator Reference

**Source:** Independently verified 2026-07-27 (see project-spec.txt Section 2
and the closing citations for the correction history — an earlier draft of
this project over-attributed all five typology names to a single FinCEN
advisory; that attribution has been corrected below). Real, named,
publicly-documented categories — not a proprietary insurer model.

## The Common Thread (NHCAA)

The National Health Care Anti-Fraud Association's public consumer-information
page frames health care fraud as "an intentional deception or
misrepresentation that the individual or entity makes knowing that the
misrepresentation could result in some unauthorized benefit." Every category
below is a specific way that misrepresentation shows up in billing — of the
service itself, its intensity, its date, or its necessity.

## The Five Billing-Fraud Categories

These five terms are standard, widely-used industry terminology — recognized
across OIG investigations, DOJ False Claims Act settlements, and (for
unbundling specifically) CMS's own National Correct Coding Initiative (NCCI).
They are not a formally-named taxonomy published by any single one of these
bodies; the terms themselves, however, are real and consistently used this
way across enforcement and industry literature.

1. **Phantom billing** — filing a claim for a good or service that was never
   provided to the patient at all. FinCEN's own March 2026 advisory (below)
   describes this category of claim as billing for "non-existent... medical
   care," using different wording for the same concept.
2. **Upcoding** — filing a claim for a more expensive good or service than
   the patient actually received.
3. **Unbundling** — filing multiple separate claims for goods or services
   that are supposed to be billed together as one bundled claim. CMS's NCCI
   program exists specifically to detect this pattern via code-pair edits.
4. **Double billing** — filing multiple claims for the exact same good or
   service, often on the same date with the same code and charge.
5. **Billing for exploitative, substandard, or unnecessary care** — care that
   technically occurred but was medically unnecessary, low-quality, or
   exploitative of the patient. FinCEN's advisory uses nearly this exact
   phrase — "exploitative, substandard, or unnecessary medical care" — in
   describing the fraudulent claims its red flags are meant to catch.

## The Volume-Spike Red Flag (FinCEN FIN-2026-A001)

Unlike the five categories above, this pattern **is** specifically named in
FinCEN's Advisory on Health Care Fraud Schemes Targeting Medicare, Medicaid,
and Other Federal and State Health Care Benefit Programs (FIN-2026-A001,
issued March 30, 2026). The advisory — aimed primarily at financial
institutions, consistent with FinCEN's actual mandate as a financial-crimes
enforcement agency, not a medical-coding regulator — flags "any sudden
significant increase in reimbursements" as a red flag, particularly when it
follows a change in a provider's beneficial ownership. A provider with
consistently low or moderate billing that suddenly files a large volume of
claims fits this pattern.

This is a red-flag pattern, not a formal typology name in the same sense as
the five categories above — cite it as such.

## Note on Sourcing Precision

"Diagnosis-procedure code mismatch" is a reasonable inferred symptom of
upcoding, not itself a separately named category in any of the sources
above — cite it as an inference, not a formal term, if used.
