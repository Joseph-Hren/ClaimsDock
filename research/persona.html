A day in her queue

Denise's queue looks deceptively uniform — a stack of flagged claims that could be anything from a coding error to a real fraud pattern. Her actual skill isn't processing volume, it's telling apart a claim that's flagged because something's genuinely wrong from one that's flagged because a rule tripped on a technicality. A good chunk of her day goes to claims that look complicated on the surface and resolve in minutes once she's actually read the documentation — and a smaller number that look routine but turn out to hide something real. The difference is rarely visible from the worklist alone; she has to go looking for it, claim by claim.

Goals

  • Get through her real caseload without losing time to claims that only look ambiguous until she's read them.
  • Be able to stand behind a decision if an auditor or a member's appeal comes back to it months later.
  • Catch a genuine bad actor without relying on memory or gut feel alone across dozens of similar-looking claims.
  • Not miss an SLA deadline she didn't realize was close, buried in a queue sorted by something other than urgency.

Frustrations

  • Can't tell which claims are safe to skim without reading each one closely.
  • Has no way to check an automated recommendation's work before deciding whether to trust it.
  • Feels like she has to accept or reject a finding wholesale — no middle ground for "the flag is right, the math is off."
  • Worries a required review step is a rubber stamp if nothing actually forces real scrutiny.
"I don't need the system to make the call for me. I need it to show its work — so when I make the call, I can actually defend it." Synthesized to represent a realistic point of view — not a verbatim interview quote. No claims adjusters were interviewed for this project.
Why her skepticism isn't a character quirk Denise doesn't trust or distrust automated recommendations uniformly — she calibrates, verifying the ones she can check and leaning on the ones she can't. That's not a personality trait invented for this persona; it's a documented pattern in how people actually respond to algorithmic advice when they're given a way to check its accuracy. [1]
